Stage 1 · Perimeter & Area Foundations

Recalling Squares, Rectangles, Triangles & Circles

Before the fair can be planned, Medha must recall the basics: how far a boundary stretches (perimeter) and how much flat ground a shape actually covers (area). These four shapes are the building blocks for everything that follows.

Boundary vs. Surface

Perimeter is a length — it measures the distance around the outside edge, in units like cm or m. Area is a surface measurement — it measures how much flat space is enclosed, always in square units like cm² or m².

Square: A = s² , P = 4s
Rectangle: A = l × b , P = 2(l + b)
Triangle: A = ½ × base × height
Circle: A = πr² , C = 2πr s = side, l = length, b = breadth, r = radius. Use π ≈ 3.14 or 22/7.
  • Square & rectangle: everyday flat surfaces — floor tiles, notebook pages, a cricket pitch.
  • Triangle: the height must always be measured perpendicular to the chosen base, not along a slanted side.
  • Circle: wheels, clock faces, circular flower beds — every point on the edge is the same distance (r) from the centre.

Solved Exercise 1.1 — Same Perimeter, Different Area

Problem: A square garden and a rectangular garden both have a perimeter of 40 m. The rectangle's length is 12 m. Which garden has more area?

Square: side = 40 ÷ 4 = 10 m → Area = 10 × 10 = 100 m² Rectangle: 2(l+b) = 40 → b = 20 − 12 = 8 m → Area = 12 × 8 = 96 m²

Result: The square garden wins (100 m² vs 96 m²) — for a fixed perimeter, a square always encloses the most area among all rectangles!

Parallel Sides & Perpendicular Height

A trapezium has exactly one pair of parallel sides. Its area is simply the average of the two parallel sides, multiplied by the perpendicular distance between them. A general quadrilateral has no such shortcut — so we split it into two triangles along a diagonal instead.

Trapezium: A = ½ × (a + b) × h
Quadrilateral (via diagonal d): A = ½ × d × (h₁ + h₂) a, b = parallel sides; h = height between them; h₁, h₂ = perpendicular offsets of the other two vertices from diagonal d.
  • Identify the parallel sides: the two edges that never meet, like the sloped boundary of a field.
  • Height, not slant: h is always measured straight across, perpendicular to both parallel sides.
  • Splitting a quadrilateral: one diagonal turns any four-sided plot into two easy triangles whose areas simply add.

Solved Exercise 2.1 — Trapezium-Shaped Field

Problem: A farmer's field is trapezium-shaped with parallel sides 60 m and 40 m, and the perpendicular distance between them is 25 m. Find its area.

A = ½ × (60 + 40) × 25 = ½ × 100 × 25 = 1250 m²

Result: The field covers 1250 m² — that's 0.125 hectares (1 hectare = 10,000 m²).

Stage 3 · Area of Rhombus & Polygons

Kite Diagonals & Polygon Patterns

A diamond-shaped kite stall catches Medha's eye. Its diagonals cross at right angles — and that one fact unlocks a shortcut for the area of every rhombus and kite at the fair.

Diagonals That Cross at Right Angles

In a rhombus (and a kite), the two diagonals always bisect each other at 90°. That lets us build the shape from four identical right triangles, giving a clean shortcut formula. Regular polygons follow a similar idea — slice them from the centre into equal triangles.

Rhombus / Kite: A = ½ × d₁ × d₂
Regular polygon (n sides, side s): A ≈ (n × s²) ÷ (4 × tan(π/n)) d₁, d₂ = diagonals. A polygon can always be estimated by slicing it into n congruent triangles from its centre.
  • Diagonals bisect at 90°: that right angle is exactly why ½ × d₁ × d₂ works.
  • Kites and rhombi share a formula: both are built from two pairs of congruent right triangles.
  • Any regular polygon can be sliced from its centre into n congruent isosceles triangles to estimate area.

Solved Exercise 3.1 — Kite-Shaped Flower Bed

Problem: A kite-shaped flower bed has diagonals of 16 m and 12 m. Turf costs ₹45 per m². Find the total cost.

Area = ½ × 16 × 12 = 96 m² Cost = 96 × ₹45 = ₹4,320

Result: The gardener needs to budget ₹4,320 for turf.

Flat Faces vs. Every Face

Lateral (or curved) surface area covers only the side walls — the faces you'd wrap with a label. Total surface area adds the top and bottom back in, which matters when you're painting, wrapping, or covering a solid completely.

Cube: LSA = 4a² , TSA = 6a²
Cuboid: LSA = 2h(l+b) , TSA = 2(lb+bh+hl)
Cylinder: CSA = 2πrh , TSA = 2πr(r+h) a = side, l/b/h = dimensions, r = radius of cylinder base.
  • Lateral surface skips the top and bottom caps — think of just the "wraparound" part.
  • Total surface puts both caps back — essential for paint, wrapping paper, or sheet metal.
  • Unfolding into a net is the fastest way to spot which faces repeat and which are unique.

Solved Exercise 4.1 — Painting a Water Tank

Problem: A cuboidal overhead tank measures 2 m × 1.5 m × 1.2 m. Find the total surface area to be painted.

TSA = 2(lb+bh+hl) = 2(2×1.5 + 1.5×1.2 + 1.2×2) = 2(3 + 1.8 + 2.4) = 2 × 7.2 = 14.4 m²

Result: The painter must cover 14.4 m² of surface.

Stage 5 · Volume, Capacity & Efficiency

Filling Space — Volume & the Best Container

At the lemonade stand, Medha wonders which container holds more for the same amount of material. Volume measures the space a solid occupies — and it decides who wins the fair's container design contest.

Space Enclosed, Not Just Covered

Volume measures the three-dimensional space a solid fills, in cubic units. Capacity measures how much a container can hold, usually in litres — a cube of side 10 cm holds exactly 1 litre.

Cube: V = a³   Cuboid: V = l×b×h   Cylinder: V = πr²h
1 L = 1000 cm³ = 1000 mL  ·  1 m³ = 1000 L Always convert every dimension to the same unit before multiplying.
  • Volume is measured in cubic units — cm³, m³ — because three dimensions multiply together.
  • Capacity converts that same space into litres or millilitres for liquids and containers.
  • Same material, different shape: a cylinder often holds more volume than a cuboid or cube built from the same amount of sheet material — that's why so many cans are cylindrical.

Solved Exercise 5.1 — Filling a Cylindrical Tank

Problem: A cylindrical tank has radius 1.4 m and height 3 m. How many litres can it hold? (π ≈ 22/7)

V = πr²h = (22/7) × 1.4² × 3 = (22/7) × 1.96 × 3 = 18.48 m³ 18.48 m³ × 1000 = 18,480 L

Result: The tank holds 18,480 litres of water.

🌍 Real-World Impact

Mensuration Across Careers & Technology

From reading architectural blueprints to surveying farmland and designing packaging, mensuration turns raw measurements into real decisions.

🏗️

Architecture & Construction

Every blueprint depends on precise area and volume calculations before a single brick is laid.

  • Estimating tiles and flooring from room area
  • Calculating paint and plaster from wall surface area
  • Costing concrete from structural volume
  • Planning roof slant heights and staircases
🌾

Agriculture & Land Surveying

Irregular fields are mapped into trapeziums and triangles to measure land and plan irrigation.

  • Mapping field boundaries into trapeziums & triangles
  • Estimating crop yield per hectare
  • Sizing irrigation canals and water tanks
  • Calculating seed and fertilizer quantity from land area
📦

Packaging & Product Design

Engineers balance surface area (material cost) against volume (capacity) for every container.

  • Designing cans and bottles that hold more for less material
  • Optimizing carton dimensions for shipping
  • Costing material from a product's unfolded net
  • Estimating volume in 3D-printed objects
